L15@P0: Abstract. This paper addresses the issues in designing the so-called ‘Controlled Korean’
L16@P0: for a Korean-English MT system. Controlled Language is a sublanguage of a natural
L17@P0: language which is supposed to improve the readability and the translatability of a text.
L18@P0: Much effort has been made to design a controlled language for major international
L19@P0: languages such as English, German, Spanish and etc. However, little effort has been made
L20@P0: yet to design a Controlled Korean in the context of machine translation. In this paper we
L21@P0: introduce the concept of the Controlled Korean we have developed for a Korean-English
L22@P0: MT system and compare the Controlled Korean with the Controlled English and Controlled
L23@P0: German from the perspective of the translatability. The result of our experiments shows
L24@P0: that in designing a Controlled Language, not only the linguistic characteristics of the
L25@P0: language but also the characteristics of an MT-System must be taken into account.
L26@P0: Keywords: Controlled Language, Controlled Korean, Machine Translation, Translatability,
L27@P0: Negative Translatability Indicators
L28@P0: 1. Introduction
L29@P0: Controlled Language is a sublanguage of a natural language designed to improve the readability
L30@P0: and the translatability of a text. Originally, the concept of a controlled language was introduced
L31@P0: in the field of technical documentations to prevent the misunderstanding of texts. 1
L32@P0:  In the last
L33@P0: couple of years the necessity of a controlled language has increased significantly, especially in
L34@P0: the technical documentation domain, as the number of pages to be translated has increased
L35@P0: enormously.
L36@P0: The increase of the volume of the documents to be translated made it necessary to employ a
L37@P0: full automatic translation system like an MT or a machine-aided translation system like
L38@P0: translation memory. In Korea, however, MT systems have not been widely welcomed by the
L39@P0: experts in the localization business, because the quality of the translations failed to match their
L40@P0: expectations.
L41@P0: The recent researches on the controlled languages show that the use of a controlled language
L42@P0: can generally lead to the improvement of machine translation quality, thus reducing the cost of
L43@P0:

L45@P0:  cf. Esselink(2000), Göpferich(1995)

L47@P0-1: 22nd Pacific Asia Conference on Language, Information and Computation, pages 391–396post-editing. The general assumption behind the idea of the controlled language is that the cost
L48@P1: of the use of a controlled language that can be paraphrased as ‘pre-editing’ is lower than that of
L49@P1: ‘post-editing’ in using an MT system for localization.
L50@P1: Previous works on the controlled language in the context of MT have mainly focused on the
L51@P1: impact of the controlled language on MT. Aikawa et al. (2007), for example, addressed the
L52@P1: impact of the controlled English for MSR-MT. Lehrndorfer (1996), Lehrndorfer&Schachtl
L53@P1: (1998) dealt with the controlled German for German-English MT.
L54@P1: In this paper, we will introduce the concept of the ‘Controlled Korean’ for Korean-English
L55@P1: MT. We will not only show the impact of the Controlled Korean on Korean-English MT, but
L56@P1: also share our experience in designing the Controlled Korean.
L57@P1: 2. Controlled Korean
L58@P1: 2.1.Background
L59@P1: Since early 2000, MT has been paid much attention in Korea because of the fast growth of the
L60@P1: Internet. However, most of the efforts to bring out the off-the-shelf products into the market
L61@P1: have failed mainly due to the difficulty in Korean syntactic parsing and the failure in the domain
L62@P1: adaptation.2
L63@P1:  One of the few Korean-English MT systems actively used not only in Korea but
L64@P1: also in foreign countries is the Korean-English Patent MT system developed at ETRI.3
L65@P1:  The
L66@P1: performance of the patent MT system is good enough for foreign patent examiners to retrieve
L67@P1: the patent documents of their interest in English.
L68@P1: The quality of the Korean-English patent MT is good enough for cross-language information
L69@P1: retrieval, however not good enough, if it is to be used for other purposes like academic paper
L70@P1: authoring. If an MT system is to be used for academic paper writing in English, the quality of
L71@P1: the translation must be far superior to that of the patent MT system, the purpose of which is not
L72@P1: to produce a perfect translation, but to produce a translation just enough for the understanding.
L73@P1: However, we are very well aware of the difficulties and obstacles in improving the performance
L74@P1: of an MT system in a short period of time. One way to improve the translation quality is to
L75@P1: employ a controlled language for MT. Kim et al. (2007) showed that even some simple writing
L76@P1: rules can improve the translation quality for around 10 %.
L77@P1: 2.2.Designing Controlled Korean
L78@P1: To design a controlled language for MT, the purpose of the application must be properly
L79@P1: understood. Most of the controlled languages introduced so far have without exceptions limited
L80@P1: lexicons and writing rules. This is possible and useful, when the controlled language and the
L81@P1: MT system are used by a homogeneous group of users like employees in a company. However,
L82@P1: in the academic paper authoring setting, the scenario should be somewhat different, i.e., the
L83@P1: users have all the different interests of their own. Thus, it is almost impossible to enforce them
L84@P1: to use only the allowed lexicon and not to use any words they like. We therefore gave up
L85@P1: introducing the restricted Korean lexicon and rather focussed on the writing rules which impact
L86@P1: on the translation quality most.
L87@P1: The philosophy in designing the Controlled Korean was to give the authors as much freedom
L88@P1: as possible. The authors write an English academic paper that will be automatically checked
L89@P1: against Controlled Korean writing rules by a Controlled Korean Checker.4
L90@P1:  Thus we investigated
L91@P1: on the translation errors that might be occurred by stylistic errors that are hard for an MT system
L92@P1: to treat properly.
L93@P1: To do this, we manually checked 40,000 Korean-English machine translation pairs. We scored
L94@P1: all the translations from 0(poor) to 4(very good). The translations above 3 points(good) were

L153@P3: 3.1.The impact of Controlled Korean on MT
L154@P3: O’Brien(2005) introduced the concept of the ‘negative translatability indicators’. The negative
L155@P3: translatability indicators are the specific linguistic constructions or phenomena that negatively
L156@P3: effect on the quality of MT. We performed an experiment to find out the negative translatability
L157@P3: indicators in Korean-English MT. We collected 50 sentences for each error type in the table 1.
L158@P3: Each sentence was rewritten only as the Controlled Korean Checker suggests, i.e., even if the
L159@P3: sentence contained the error of the given type, if it is not detected by our Controlled Korean
L160@P3: Checker, the sentence was not corrected. Table 2 shows the result of our experiment.

L172@P3: It turned out that the ’ambiguous expressions’, ’subject-predicate mismatch’, and ’double
L173@P3: subject/object construction’ error types are the most important negative translatability indicators.
L174@P3: Most of these error types were related to resolving ambiguities in the analysis. As mentioned, in
L175@P3: order to detect the subject-predicate semantic mismatch, many rules on the lexical level must be
L176@P3: written. In other words, the more lexical rules we have, the better is the the performance of the
L177@P3: Controlled Korean Checker, hence Korean-English MT system expected to be.
L178@P3: The overall performance of the Korean-English MT system supported by the Controlled
L179@P3: Korean Checker was improved for 4.25%. As Kim et al.(2007) showed, if we can provide the
L180@P3: checker with more rules, we can improve the performance of the MT system for more than 10%.

L233@P4: 4. Conclusion
L234@P4: In this paper we proposed ‘Controlled Korean’ for Korean-English MT. We also shared our
L235@P4: experience in designing a controlled language. We didn’t introduce the controlled lexicon as
L236@P4: done in many other researches, but we focused on the writing rules. As the learnability is a very
L237@P4: important factor for the acceptability by the users, we tried to formalize the Controlled Korean
L238@P4: rules as much as possible. As a result, we could improve the translation accuracy of the KoreanEnglish MT system backed up by a Controlled Korean Checker for 4.25%.
L239@P4: Another important issue in designing a controlled language for MT is whether the MT
L240@P4: paradigm should be considered. Our experiment and the comparison of our result with Aikawa
L241@P4: et al.(2007) showed that the MT paradigm could play a very important role in designing a
L242@P4: controlled language for MT. Our experiment with Controlled German for rule-based MT
L243@P4: showed different results from that of Aikawa et al.(2007). For a rule-based MT, ambiguitiesresolving rules seem to play important roles, whereas for a statistics-based MT, rather the rules
